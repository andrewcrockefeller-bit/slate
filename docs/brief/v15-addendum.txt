DECISIONS TAKEN AS INPUT
------------------------
  - v1.5 user is the SAME student, other classes. The engineering student who
    used v1 for calculus now uses it for their composition paper and their
    history reading. This is the "one canvas for all my homework" play, not an
    expansion into a humanities audience.
  - Handwriting is for thinking; typed text is for prose. Outlining,
    annotation, and argument-mapping stay on the canvas because they are
    genuinely better by hand. Paragraph drafting happens in a typed surface,
    because nobody writes a five-page paper with a Pencil.


=============================================================================
0. READ THIS SECTION EVEN IF YOU READ NOTHING ELSE
=============================================================================

Planning v1.5 now changes what v1 must build. Six model decisions have to be
made correctly in M2 and M3 or v1.5 becomes a data migration against real user
data — the exact failure Invariant 4 exists to prevent. They are listed in
section 8 and mirrored into the v1 brief as section 10.

The expensive one, stated here because it is the easiest to skip: **when v1
imports a PDF or photo, it must extract and store the text alongside the
backdrop image, even though v1 never reads it.** v1 uses the image. v1.5 needs
the text. Extraction at import time is nearly free. Backfilling it across every
document a user has already created is a background migration job you will
write at the worst possible moment.


=============================================================================
1. THE THROUGH-LINE
=============================================================================

This is not a second product bolted on. It is the same loop pointed at a
different subject:

    v1    notices the moment your ALGEBRA bends
    v1.5  notices the moment your ARGUMENT bends

In both cases the tutor is watching partial work, identifying the *class* of
error, and emitting the smallest sufficient nudge. A paragraph whose topic
sentence and closing sentence make different claims is structurally the same
failure as cancelling terms instead of factors: the student has done something
locally plausible that breaks the thing globally, and cannot see it because
they are inside it.

The test for any v1.5 feature is unchanged from v1: **does removing it break
that loop?** Features that only make sense as a review pass on finished work
are out. We are not building a proofreader.

  OPINION — THE PART OF YOUR REQUEST I WANT TO PUSH BACK ON. You listed grammar
  analysis alongside literature and document analysis. It is by a wide margin
  the least valuable item on that list and the only one that is off-thesis.
  Grammar checking is a commodity — Grammarly, Apple's built-in proofreading,
  and every word processor already do it, for free, better, and inline. Building
  it wins us nothing and costs us the "not a proofreader" positioning that keeps
  this app defensible with teachers.

  I have kept it, but narrowly and deliberately reframed: no inline squiggles,
  no corrections offered mid-draft. Mechanics are surfaced only as *patterns* in
  the session summary — "you wrote four comma splices today, all of them joining
  a claim to its evidence; here's what that structure usually wants instead."
  That is something no proofreader does, it is on-thesis, and it costs almost
  nothing once the session summary exists. Section 6 has the detail. If you want
  real inline grammar checking, say so and I'll scope it, but I think it makes
  the product worse.


=============================================================================
2. WHAT v1.5 SHIPS
=============================================================================

Three surfaces, each with the tutor loop attached.

  A. THE READING SURFACE — literature and document analysis
  B. THE PLANNING SURFACE — outlining and argument mapping, by hand
  C. THE DRAFTING SURFACE — typed prose with a structural tutor

They are not three modes. A single document holds all three: the source text
on the left of the canvas, the student's handwritten annotation and outline in
the middle, the typed draft in a pane. The tutor sees all of it at once, which
is the whole advantage over three separate apps.


-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
2A. THE READING SURFACE
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHAT IT DOES
  - Imports a source text: PDF, EPUB, DOCX, plain text, a pasted article, or a
    photographed book page. Text is extracted, not just rendered.
  - Displays it as a readable pane on the canvas, reflowable, with the
    handwriting canvas alive around and over it.
  - The student annotates by hand: underlines, margin notes, brackets, arrows,
    question marks. All captured as normalized strokes anchored to text spans,
    not to screen coordinates.
  - Reading passages become work regions with state, exactly as math problems
    do in v1.

WHAT THE TUTOR DOES
  - Watches annotation as it happens. A margin note that misreads a passage is
    the reading equivalent of a wrong algebraic step, and it is catchable the
    same way.
  - Asks Socratic questions anchored to the passage the student is on, at the
    moment they slow down or stop.
  - Surfaces structure the student hasn't marked: an unmarked shift in
    narrator reliability, an unmarked counterargument, a claim the author makes
    without support.
  - Tracks a passage-level map of what the student has and hasn't engaged with,
    which is the input to the reading session summary.

WHAT IT DOES NOT DO
  - Does not summarize the text. Ever. A summary is the answer to a reading
    assignment in the same way a solved equation is the answer to a math
    problem. This is the R6 line in section 4 and it is the single highest
    cheating-risk surface in the product.
  - Does not answer comprehension questions.
  - Does not tell the student what a passage means. It asks what they think it
    means and then presses on the weak part of that.

  OPINION — ANCHOR ANNOTATIONS TO TEXT SPANS, NOT COORDINATES. This is the
  non-obvious engineering decision in the reading surface and it needs to be
  right the first time. If a handwritten underline is stored at canvas
  coordinates, it breaks the moment the text reflows — different font size,
  different device, rotation. Annotations must be anchored to a character range
  in the extracted text, with the canvas position derived at render time. That
  makes the anchor a pure-Swift value type (document ID + character range +
  stroke geometry relative to the span), which lives in Layer 1 and tests on
  Linux. It also means an annotation survives being viewed on a phone, which
  Invariant 5 requires and coordinate anchoring makes impossible.


-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
2B. THE PLANNING SURFACE
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------

This is the part that most justifies the app existing, and the part no
competitor does. Students plan essays badly because planning is invisible work
with no feedback until the paper comes back graded.

WHAT IT DOES
  - Handwritten outlining on the infinite canvas: bullets, indentation, arrows
    between ideas, boxes, crossing things out and moving them.
  - Argument mapping: the student marks claims, evidence, and the link between
    them. Lightweight — a gesture or a region kind, not a formal notation the
    student has to learn.
  - A thesis region, marked once, that everything else is evaluated against.

WHAT THE TUTOR DOES
  - Watches the outline take shape and flags structural problems while they are
    still cheap to fix:
        - a body section with no evident connection to the thesis
        - two sections making the same claim in different words
        - a claim with no evidence attached
        - evidence attached to no claim
        - an ordering where section 3 depends on something not established
          until section 5
        - a thesis that is a topic, not an argument (the most common failure
          in student writing, and trivially detectable)
  - Asks what the counterargument is, if the student hasn't marked one.
  - Never proposes the outline. Never fills in a missing section.

  OPINION: "your thesis is a topic, not a claim" is the single highest-value
  intervention in the entire writing side of this product, it fires on nearly
  every first draft, and it is detectable from about fifteen words of student
  handwriting. If v1.5 shipped with only that one behavior it would still be
  worth shipping.


-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
2C. THE DRAFTING SURFACE
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHAT IT DOES
  - A typed text pane, first-class on the canvas, positioned beside the outline
    so the student can see their plan while drafting.
  - Hardware keyboard and on-screen keyboard. Standard text editing.
  - Sections of the draft are linked to outline nodes, so the tutor knows which
    part of the plan a given paragraph is supposed to be executing.
  - Live word count against a target, because assignments have one.

WHAT THE TUTOR DOES
  - Structural and argumentative feedback only, at paragraph granularity or
    coarser:
        - paragraph drifts from its own topic sentence
        - claim asserted without the evidence the outline promised
        - a transition that asserts a logical relationship the content does not
          support ("therefore" between two unrelated observations)
        - evidence quoted but never interpreted — the most common undergraduate
          failure after the topic-thesis problem
        - a paragraph that has drifted off the outline node it is linked to
        - the draft has stopped tracking the thesis
  - Fires on paragraph completion and on stall, not on keystroke. Nothing about
    this should feel like a spell checker.

WHAT IT DOES NOT DO — see R6, section 4
  - Does not write, rewrite, or complete sentences beyond a single
    demonstration sentence at hint Level 4.
  - Does not generate a thesis, a topic sentence, a transition, or a
    conclusion.
  - Has no autocomplete, no "continue writing", no suggested phrasing.

3. THE WRITING HINT LADDER
=============================================================================

The v1 ladder is math-shaped. It generalizes, but the levels have to be
redefined for text, not reused. Worked below against one real failure: a
paragraph whose topic sentence claims the narrator is unreliable and whose
closing sentence claims he is naive.

  LEVEL 1 — ORIENT
    Points at the location. Names no craft concept.
    "Read your second paragraph's first and last sentences back to back.
     Something moves between them."

  LEVEL 2 — PRINCIPLE
    Names the general rule, in the abstract, without touching their text.
    "A paragraph should advance one claim. When the opening and closing
     sentences argue different things, the reader can't tell which one the
     evidence was supposed to support."

  LEVEL 3 — APPLIED NUDGE
    Applies it to their actual paragraph and poses the next decision. Stops
    before making it.
    "You open saying the narrator is unreliable and close saying he's naive —
     those are different arguments. Which one is this paragraph making? Once
     you pick, which of your three quotes actually supports that one?"

  LEVEL 4 — ONE DEMONSTRATION
    Rewrites exactly ONE sentence, as a demonstration of the move, and stops.
    Never a paragraph. Never the thesis. Never the sentence the student is
    currently writing.
    "Here's what a single-claim version of your topic sentence looks like:
     'The narrator's account of the dinner cannot be taken at face value.'
     Notice it commits to one reading. Now do the closing sentence yourself."
    Requires: explicit student request AND two prior revisions of this
    paragraph. Never fires from a stall.

DIFFERENCES FROM THE MATH LADDER, WHICH MATTER
  - There is no "correct answer" to withhold. The failure mode is not leaking a
    solution; it is producing prose the student can submit. That makes the
    constraint stricter, not looser — see R6.
  - Levels 1 and 2 can fire on things that are not errors. A paragraph can be
    correct and still weak. The math ladder only fires on wrongness; the
    writing ladder fires on weakness, which means false-positive tolerance is
    lower and `confidenceFloor` should be set higher for text domains.
  - Escalation is per-paragraph, not per-problem. A student may be at Level 3
    on paragraph two and Level 1 on paragraph four simultaneously.

THE READING LADDER
  Reading is Socratic rather than corrective and inverts the shape: the tutor
  asks rather than tells at every level, and the levels differ in how much of
  the interpretive work the question does.
    L1  "What do you make of the shift in how he describes his brother here?"
    L2  "Compare how he describes his brother on this page with page 40. What
         changed, and is it the brother or the narrator that changed?"
    L3  "The description gets warmer right after the inheritance is settled.
         What does that timing suggest about what's driving his account?"
    L4  Names one piece of textual evidence the student has not engaged with,
        and asks what they make of it. Never states the interpretation.

4. R6 — THE PROSE RULE
=============================================================================

Added to the hard rules in section 3 of the v1 brief. This is stricter than
R1/R2 and here is why, stated plainly because it will be tempting to weaken:

  In math, a leaked step is a step. The student still has to assemble the rest,
  and a teacher looking at the work can see whether the student can do it.

  In writing, THE PROSE IS THE ARTIFACT. A tutor that emits a well-formed
  paragraph has not given a hint — it has produced a submittable deliverable.
  There is no partial-credit version of that. Every "help me phrase this"
  request is one keystroke from plagiarism, and the request will be constant.

RULES

  R6.1  The tutor never emits more than `maxGeneratedProseWords` of contiguous
        prose in the student's document voice. Default: 25. One sentence.
  R6.2  Level 4 demonstration sentences are the ONLY generated prose permitted,
        are capped at one per hint, and are visually and structurally marked as
        tutor output — a distinct element type that cannot be silently pasted
        into the draft as the student's own text.
  R6.3  The tutor never generates a thesis statement, a topic sentence for an
        unwritten paragraph, a transition, an introduction, or a conclusion,
        at any level, under any request. These are the load-bearing sentences;
        writing them IS writing the paper.
  R6.4  The tutor never summarizes an assigned source text, and never answers a
        comprehension question about it.
  R6.5  No autocomplete, no continuation, no "improve this" rewrite of student
        prose. If the student asks for a rewrite, the response is a Level 2 or
        3 hint about what is weak, not a rewrite.
  R6.6  Copying tutor-generated prose into the draft is a tracked event. It
        does not block, but it appears in the session summary and, if a teacher
        integration ever exists, it is the thing that makes this app trustable.

  R6 is enforced by a Layer 1 validator on parsed responses, the same mechanism
  as R1/R2, with a text-domain rule set. `enforceNoProse` is a feature flag that
  ships true and never ships false.

  OPINION: R6.2's "distinct element type" is worth the extra work. A
  demonstration sentence that lives as a tutor-owned canvas object rather than
  as selectable text in the draft makes the lazy path — select, copy, paste —
  meaningfully harder than the intended path. Not impossible. Harder. That
  asymmetry is most of what product-level integrity enforcement can actually
  achieve, and it is worth more than any detection system.

5. TRIGGER EVENTS FOR TEXT WORK
=============================================================================

The v1 triggers assume the student is producing strokes in a bounded region.
Reading produces almost no strokes, and typing produces a continuous stream.
Both need their own trigger sets. T1's stall semantics in particular do NOT
carry over: a student who has stopped writing while reading is *reading*, which
is the desired behavior, not a stall.

READING TRIGGERS
  RT1  PASSAGE DWELL — reading position stationary on one passage beyond
       `passageDwellThreshold`, with no annotation. Distinguished from normal
       reading by the absence of scroll progress.
  RT2  REGRESSION — student scrolls back to a passage already read.
       The strongest confusion signal available in reading, and the analog of
       v1's erase burst. Highest-value reading trigger.
  RT3  ANNOTATION EVENT — student marks, underlines, or writes a margin note.
       Evaluate the note against the passage.
  RT4  QUESTION MARK — student writes "?" in a margin. Explicit ask. Never
       suppressed.
  RT5  PASSAGE COMPLETE — student moves past a passage marked as required.
       Evaluation, not a hint: records engagement for the summary.

WRITING TRIGGERS
  WT1  PARAGRAPH COMPLETE — a paragraph break followed by
       `paragraphSettleDelay` of no typing. The primary writing trigger.
  WT2  TYPING STALL — no keystroke for `typingStallThreshold` mid-paragraph.
       Signals stuck, not finished. Longer than v1's stall; composition pauses
       are normal and interrupting them is worse than being late.
  WT3  DELETION BURST — more than `deletionBurstFraction` of recent characters
       deleted. The typed analog of the erase burst.
  WT4  OUTLINE DIVERGENCE — a drafted section's content stops matching the
       outline node it is linked to. Fires at most once per section.
  WT5  EXPLICIT ASK — the hint affordance, or "?" written on the canvas.

SUPPRESSION
  All v1 suppression rules carry over. Two additions:
  - Never fire WT1 or WT2 while the student is mid-sentence by punctuation, not
    just mid-keystroke. Interrupting a sentence in progress is the single most
    destructive thing this product can do to a writer.
  - Reading triggers are capped much lower than math triggers.
    `maxHintsPerPassage` defaults to 3. Reading tolerates interruption far
    worse than problem-solving does.


=============================================================================
6. MECHANICS AND GRAMMAR — DELIBERATELY NARROW
=============================================================================

Not inline. Not corrective. Not while drafting.

WHAT SHIPS
  - Mechanics errors are detected and recorded silently during the session,
    never surfaced in the moment.
  - The session summary reports them as PATTERNS with structural context, not
    as a list of corrections:
      "Six comma splices today. Five of them joined a claim to the evidence for
       it — that's usually a signal you want a colon or a subordinating
       conjunction, because the second clause is explaining the first rather
       than adding to it."
      "You used passive voice in every sentence where you attributed a claim to
       a source. Worth checking whether that's hedging."
  - Repeated patterns across sessions become a tracked profile, once
    cross-session modeling exists.

WHY THIS SHAPE
  - Inline grammar checking is a commodity the student already has, and it
    trains them to fix symptoms without seeing the pattern.
  - Pattern-level reporting is genuinely differentiated, is on-thesis, and is
    nearly free once the session summary infrastructure from v1's M8 exists.
  - It keeps the product out of the "AI writing assistant" category, which is
    the category that gets banned.

WHAT DOES NOT SHIP
  - Squiggly underlines, inline corrections, one-tap fixes, style scoring,
    readability grades. All of it is off-thesis and all of it dilutes the
    positioning.

7. DOCUMENT INGESTION ARCHITECTURE
=============================================================================

This is the genuinely new technical problem in v1.5 and it is mostly a
Layer 1 problem, which is good news for the invariants.

THE PROBLEM
  A math problem region rasterizes to one image that fits in one request. A
  novel does not. A 300-page assigned text cannot be sent to a provider on
  every trigger, and the student's key would not survive it if it could.

THE SHAPE
  Layer 2 does extraction and I/O only:
    `TextExtracting`      PDF / EPUB / DOCX / OCR → plain text + structure
    `DocumentImporting`   file access, format sniffing
  Layer 1 does everything else, in pure Swift, tested on Linux:
    - Structural parsing: chapters, sections, paragraphs, character offsets
    - Chunking into retrievable units with stable IDs
    - Relevance selection: given the student's current passage, annotations,
      thesis, and draft, choose which chunks go into the evaluation context
    - Context budgeting: pack the selected chunks under a configured token
      ceiling, with documented priority ordering

  OPINION — RETRIEVAL SELECTION BELONGS IN LAYER 1 AND THIS IS A WIN, NOT A
  BURDEN. It is the part most likely to be tuned repeatedly, it is pure
  deterministic logic over text, and having it Linux-testable means you can
  iterate on retrieval quality with fast unit tests instead of by hand on a
  device. Do not let it drift into Layer 2 because "the embedding model lives
  there." If semantic embedding is used at all, the embedding *call* is a Layer
  1 protocol with a Layer 2 adapter, exactly like AIProvider; the *selection
  logic* over returned vectors stays in Layer 1.

  Start with lexical and structural retrieval — the student's current passage
  plus neighbors, plus chunks containing terms from their annotations and
  thesis. Add embeddings only if that measurably fails. It probably won't for
  a single assigned text, and it adds a dependency, a cost, and a storage
  format we would have to version.

EVALUATION CONTEXT FOR TEXT DOMAINS
  - Reading: current passage text + neighbors + the student's annotations on
    them, anchored by character range + prior hints + passage state.
  - Writing: thesis + outline structure + the paragraph in question + adjacent
    paragraphs + the outline node this paragraph is linked to + prior hints.
  - Handwritten annotation is sent both as rasterized ink (it is handwriting;
    the v1 image path already works) and as its text-span anchor, so the model
    knows exactly what the note is attached to.

8. WHAT v1 MUST CHANGE NOW
=============================================================================

Six changes to the v1 milestone plan. All are cheap now and expensive later.
None of them add a v1 feature; they only prevent a v1.5 migration.

  C1. CANVAS ELEMENTS ARE POLYMORPHIC FROM M2.
      The document model is a collection of `CanvasElement`, not a collection
      of strokes. Kinds in v1: `.strokeGroup`, `.image`, `.hint`. Kinds added
      in v1.5: `.textBlock`, `.sourceDocument`, `.outlineNode`. Every element
      carries UUID + lastModified + owner regardless of kind.
      Cost now: an enum and a protocol. Cost later: migrating every document
      every user has made.

  C2. `ProblemRegion` IS NAMED `WorkRegion` AND CARRIES A `kind` FROM M3.
      v1 ships one kind, `.mathProblem`. v1.5 adds `.readingPassage`,
      `.outlineSection`, `.draftSection`. Region state
      (untouched / in-progress / solved / set-aside) generalizes cleanly;
      "solved" becomes "complete".
      Cost now: a better name and one enum. Cost later: a rename across the
      codebase plus a schema migration.

  C3. THE LADDER IS A PROTOCOL, NOT HARDCODED LOGIC, FROM M6.
      `HintLadder` declares the four levels, escalation rules, and validation
      hooks. `MathLadder` conforms in v1. `WritingLadder` and `ReadingLadder`
      conform in v1.5 without touching the tutor state machine. Per Invariant 3
      this is what the seam is supposed to look like.

  C4. `EvaluationContext` IS COMPOSABLE FROM M5.
      Not "an image plus metadata." A structure that can carry
      `.rasterizedRegion`, and later `.textSpan`, `.documentChunks`,
      `.outlineGraph`, without the pipeline in section 5 of the v1 brief
      changing shape.

  C5. ERROR CLASSES ARE NAMESPACED BY DOMAIN FROM THE START.
      Resolves Q4 in the v1 brief. `math.cancellation.termsNotFactors`,
      `writing.paragraph.claimDrift`, `reading.inference.unsupported`. Flat
      taxonomies do not survive a second domain.

  C6. IMPORTED DOCUMENTS STORE EXTRACTED TEXT FROM M7.
      v1 imports a PDF or photo as a backdrop image and never reads the text.
      Extract and store it anyway, with character offsets and page mapping.
      v1.5 requires it, extraction at import is nearly free, and backfilling it
      across existing user documents is a migration job written under pressure.

  Two of these — C2 and C5 — are pure naming. They cost nothing today and are
  the ones most likely to get skipped as premature. Do them.

9. CONFIGURATION ADDITIONS — TutorConfig
=============================================================================

Added to the v1 config object in section 7 of the founding brief.

  passageDwellThreshold      45.0 s   RT1 threshold. Long — reading slowly is
                                      not being stuck.
  regressionSensitivity       1       Scroll-backs to a read passage before
                                      RT2 fires.
  maxHintsPerPassage          3       Much lower than the math cap. Reading
                                      tolerates interruption badly.
  paragraphSettleDelay        3.0 s   WT1 delay after a paragraph break.
  typingStallThreshold       25.0 s   WT2. Long by design — composition pauses
                                      are normal and healthy.
  deletionBurstFraction       0.35    WT3 trigger.
  deletionBurstWindow        15.0 s   Window for the above.
  maxGeneratedProseWords     25       R6.1 hard cap on contiguous generated
                                      prose. Do not raise this.
  textConfidenceFloor         0.75    Higher than v1's 0.60. Writing hints fire
                                      on weakness rather than wrongness, so a
                                      false positive costs more.
  contextTokenCeiling      12000      Retrieval packing budget per evaluation.
                                      The main cost lever for text work.
  chunkTargetTokens          400      Retrieval chunk size.
  chunkNeighborRadius         2       Chunks either side of the current passage
                                      always included.
  enforceNoProse           true       R6 validator flag. Never ships false.
  outlineDivergenceOn      true       Feature flag on WT4 — the most
                                      speculative trigger in the set.

10. MILESTONES M9-M14
=============================================================================

Continuing from v1's M8. Each ends runnable on a physical iPad.

  M9 — TEXT AS A FIRST-CLASS ELEMENT
    `.textBlock` canvas elements. Typed entry, hardware and on-screen keyboard,
    selection, standard editing. Text blocks persist through the same
    repository and ordered-operation path as strokes.
    You should see: type a paragraph on the canvas beside your handwriting,
    force-quit, relaunch, it's all there.

  M10 — DOCUMENT INGESTION
    `TextExtracting` and `DocumentImporting` adapters. Structural parsing,
    chunking, and retrieval selection in Layer 1 with Linux tests. A reader
    pane that displays an imported text.
    You should see: import a PDF chapter, read it in a proper reflowing pane,
    and a debug view showing which chunks retrieval would select for the
    passage you're on.

  M11 — ANNOTATION AND THE READING LOOP
    Span-anchored handwritten annotation. Reading passages as work regions.
    Triggers RT1-RT5. `ReadingLadder`.
    You should see: underline a sentence and write a margin note that
    misreads it; the tutor asks you a question about it without telling you
    you're wrong.

  M12 — PLANNING AND THE WRITING LADDER
    Outline nodes, thesis region, claim/evidence marking, outline-to-draft
    linking. `WritingLadder`. Triggers WT1-WT5. The R6 validator.
    You should see: write a thesis that's a topic rather than a claim, and get
    told so within seconds — without being told what your thesis should be.

  M13 — MECHANICS PATTERNS AND THE TEXT SESSION SUMMARY
    Silent mechanics detection. Pattern-level summary. Reading engagement map.
    Copy-from-tutor tracking (R6.6).
    You should see: finish a drafting session and get a summary that names a
    habit you didn't know you had.

  M14 — INTEGRATION AND SHIP
    One document holding source text, handwritten outline, and typed draft,
    with the tutor seeing all three. Performance pass on long documents.
    TestFlight.
    You should see: your whole paper — reading, planning, drafting — in one
    canvas, with a tutor that knows your draft is drifting from the passage you
    annotated an hour ago. That is the thing no other product does.


=============================================================================
11. OPEN QUESTIONS
=============================================================================

  Q6.  Cost. Text work sends far more tokens per evaluation than a rasterized
       math region, and writing triggers fire more often than math triggers.
       A drafting session could plausibly cost several times a math session on
       the student's own key. Measure at M10 before tuning
       `contextTokenCeiling` and the trigger thresholds.
  Q7.  Does the reading loop actually work? v1's core risk was whether a model
       reads handwriting well enough to diagnose from, and M4 answers it. The
       equivalent v1.5 risk is whether the tutor can tell a genuinely insightful
       margin note from a wrong one without the source's critical apparatus.
       Unproven. M11 is the go/no-go, and it should be treated as one.
  Q8.  Copyright and assigned texts. Sending chunks of a copyrighted textbook
       to a provider is the student's own use of their own key, which is
       probably fine, but "probably" is doing work there. Worth a real look
       before M10, not after.
  Q9.  Handwritten annotation OCR quality on marginalia. Margin notes are the
       messiest handwriting a person produces — small, cramped, hurried. v1's
       M4 proves handwriting reading on worked math, which is comparatively
       neat. This may not transfer.
  Q10. Does the "same student, other classes" bet hold? It is the premise of
       this whole addendum. The cheapest possible test is asking three real
       students whether they would use one app for calc homework and their
       composition paper, before M9 rather than after M14.
