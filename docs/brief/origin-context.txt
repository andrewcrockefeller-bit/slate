=============================================================================
SESSION CONTEXT — ORIGIN STORY AND PROBLEM STATEMENT
"AI tutor watching a handwritten whiteboard" — what was built, what worked,
and where it broke down
=============================================================================

PURPOSE OF THIS DOCUMENT
------------------------
Background context. It describes a working prototype of an idea — an AI that
watches a student handwrite math on a tablet and tutors them in real time —
assembled out of off-the-shelf parts during a single session. It documents what
the setup was, what it proved, and the specific friction that makes a
purpose-built application worth building.

Read this as the origin story and problem statement. It is not a spec.


-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. THE SETUP THAT WAS ACTUALLY BUILT
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Participants:
  - A student (University of Florida, incoming mechanical engineering) preparing
    for the ALEKS math placement exam, which gates enrollment in Calculus 1
    (MAC 2311) and General Chemistry 1 (CHM 2045). A 76% score clears both.
  - An AI assistant (Claude, running in "Cowork" mode — a cloud-hosted agent
    session with the ability to request scoped control of applications on the
    user's Mac).

Physical arrangement:
  - Student handwrites with an Apple Pencil in Apple Freeform on an iPad.
  - Freeform syncs the board to the student's Mac over iCloud.
  - The AI was granted app-scoped control of Freeform on the Mac only. Every
    other application was excluded from its view at the compositor level.
  - The AI observed by taking periodic screenshots of the Freeform window and
    reading the student's handwriting out of the image.

What the AI did, concretely:
  a) Researched the actual placement requirement from the university registrar
     and catalog, and confirmed a single ALEKS exam covers both math and
     chemistry placement.
  b) Authored ten practice problems calibrated to the exam: five routine
     warm-ups and five chosen because they are commonly missed by students who
     have already taken calculus (extraneous log solutions, rational inequality
     sign flips, sqrt(x^2) = |x| rather than x, lost solutions from dividing
     out sin x, domain exclusions inside a radical's interval).
  c) Created the worksheet directly inside Freeform via UI automation — inserted
     a text box, typed all ten problems with Unicode math characters, set the
     font to 36pt to visually match the student's handwriting size, set left
     alignment, widened the text box so each problem fit on one line, and tuned
     the blank-line spacing between problems so each had roughly four to six
     lines of the student's actual handwriting height in which to work.
  d) Created a second, separate "TIPS" text box positioned beside the worksheet,
     and appended hints to it as the student worked.
  e) Entered an observation loop: screenshot every 30-60 seconds, read the
     student's handwritten scratch work, diagnose the error, and append a
     directional hint — never the answer.

A real exchange from the session, verbatim in substance:
  - Problem: simplify (3x^2 - 12)/(x^2 + x - 6).
  - Student's first attempt cancelled individual terms out of the numerator and
    denominator, producing nonsense.
  - AI's tip: "You can only cancel FACTORS, not terms. Factor the top and the
    bottom completely first, then see what actually matches. Right now you're
    cancelling pieces of a sum."
  - Student erased, restarted, and produced 3(x^2 - 4)/(x^2 + x - 6), then wrote
    "What's next now?" on the canvas.
  - AI's next tip: "Good — numerator is right so far. x^2 - 4 is a difference of
    squares, so factor that too. Then factor the bottom: what pair multiplies to
    -6 and adds to +1?"

Note the shape of that loop. The AI is not grading a final answer. It is reading
partially-completed handwritten work, identifying the *class* of error, and
emitting the smallest nudge that unblocks the next step.


-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. AN IMPORTANT BOUNDARY THAT WAS ESTABLISHED
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Early in the session the student asked the AI to help during the live placement
exam. The AI declined, on two grounds:

  - ALEKS explicitly requires answers based solely on the test-taker's own
    knowledge; assisting would violate the university honor code.
  - More practically, the exam is a diagnostic, not a gate to be beaten. An
    inflated score places the student into Calculus 1 and General Chemistry
    simultaneously without the algebra fluency either course assumes — a
    materially worse outcome than placing accurately and clearing the retake.

The session then pivoted to genuine preparation. This boundary is not incidental
trivia; it is a product requirement. Any tool of this kind must be explicitly
designed for practice and homework, must refuse to operate during proctored
assessment, and must lead with hints rather than solutions. A product that
positions itself as "AI that does your homework" is a cheating tool with a
short commercial life. A product that positions itself as "AI that watches you
work and catches you the moment you go wrong" is a tutor.


-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. WHAT THE PROTOTYPE PROVED
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------

  - Reading handwritten mathematical work from a screenshot is reliable enough
    to tutor from. Fractions, radicals, exponents, and mid-solution scratch work
    were all read correctly.
  - Seeing the *steps* is qualitatively more valuable than seeing the answer.
    The diagnostic signal is in where the reasoning bends, not in whether the
    final value matched.
  - A hint delivered into the same visual workspace, next to the problem, at the
    moment of being stuck, is a different experience from typing a question into
    a chat window. The student never had to describe their problem, transcribe
    their work, or context-switch out of the canvas.
  - An AI can author the worksheet, tune it to the student's handwriting size,
    and then tutor against it — content generation and tutoring are one loop,
    not two products.


-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. WHERE IT BROKE DOWN — THE CASE FOR A REAL APP
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Every one of these is an artifact of bolting an AI onto an app that has no
concept of AI. They are the requirements list in negative form.

  ARCHITECTURE
  - No direct path to the iPad. The AI could not see the tablet the student was
    actually writing on. It required a Mac running the same app, with iCloud
    syncing between them, adding seconds of lag and a hard dependency on a
    second device.
  - Observation was screenshot polling: slow, coarse, expensive, and blind
    between frames. The AI saw a static image every 30-60 seconds and had no
    access to stroke data, stroke order, timing, pressure, or the fact that the
    student had paused for ninety seconds on one line.

  LATENCY AND TRIGGERING
  - Fixed-interval polling is the wrong trigger. Real tutoring fires on
    *events*: the student stops writing for four seconds, the student erases
    something, the student finishes a problem, the student writes a question
    mark. Polling either interrupts mid-thought or arrives long after the
    student got stuck.

  BRITTLENESS OF UI AUTOMATION
  - Text boxes were inserted at the viewport center and had to be dragged into
    position afterward.
  - Font size, alignment, and box width all defaulted wrong and required
    separate corrective interactions.
  - Appending to an existing text box corrupted it, because a double-click to
    focus selected a word and the subsequent typing partially overwrote it.
  - Clicks failed intermittently because unrelated application windows sat
    invisibly above the target coordinates.
  - The application window moved between screenshots, invalidating cached
    coordinates.

  MISSING DOMAIN MODEL
  - The canvas had no notion of "problem 7." There was no per-problem state, no
    record of which problems were attempted, no history of hints already given,
    no way to know whether a hint had been read, and no way to summarize the
    session's error patterns afterward.
  - Nothing was learned across sessions. The next session starts from zero.

  MISSING INPUT PATHS
  - No way to import a homework PDF or a photograph of a textbook page and have
    problems laid out on the canvas automatically.

The through-line: the prototype worked because the *model* is good enough, and
struggled everywhere the *plumbing* was improvised. A native application with
direct access to the canvas, stroke-level input, an explicit problem model, and
an event-driven tutor loop removes essentially all of the friction above while
keeping everything that worked.


-----------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. THE PRODUCT THESIS, IN ONE PARAGRAPH
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------

A handwriting-first infinite canvas — the feel and freedom of Apple Freeform,
built for an Apple Pencil — with an AI tutor that is a native participant in the
canvas rather than a chat window bolted to the side. The student imports or is
given problems, works them by hand, and the tutor watches the strokes, notices
the moment the reasoning goes wrong, and places a hint on the canvas next to the
work. It never gives the answer first. It works with whichever frontier model
the student brings a key for. It starts on iPad, where the Pencil experience is
the product, and ports outward from there.
